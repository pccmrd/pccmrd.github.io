KERES 9

A 2D Newtonian space sim about flying your ship with your own two hands.

What is this?

Most space games ask you to click a station and wait. Kestrel 9 asks you to fly there — to bleed off your relative velocity, match the slow rotation of a docking port, and settle onto magnetic landing gear without turning your ship into confetti.

The whole game lives inside one unbroken sector of an asteroid belt, held in the gravity well of a distant moon. There are no loading screens. Station interiors, derelict hulls, and asteroid caves don't load — they simply become visible as you enter them, the way a roof disappears when you step into a house in a top-down RPG.

Around you, the sector keeps living: cargo spills when hulls crack, NPCs with their own goals and finances trade and fight and remember, and somewhere in a fog-filled crater there's a cave with something worth the risk.

What you actually do

  • Fly manually. Full Newtonian physics. Every gram of thrust costs you, and stopping is harder than going.
  • Dock like you mean it. Rotating stations, drifting wrecks, magnetic gear, gentle contact. No autopilot to hide behind.
  • Breach what won't open. Anchor to a dead hull, cut through, and board it. Not every door has a working port.
  • Explore seamless interiors. Station concourses, derelict corridors, and asteroid caves with their own biomes — all without a single loading screen.
  • Scan with skill. Directional scanning isn't a button press. Point it, work it, and read what comes back.
  • Break everything. Modular, fully destructible ships, stations, and asteroids. Guns fire from exactly where you bolted them on.
  • Live in an economy. Real cargo in real containers. Spill it, steal it, sell it. NPCs make their own moves based on their own situation.
Gritty, modular, hard-Sci-Fi pixel voxels. Ships are assemblies, not skins. Damage is geometry.
